Cold Plunge Heart Rate & Blood Pressure

Updated October 2026

Cold-water immersion can cause rapid changes in heart rate, breathing, blood vessels, and blood pressure. The strongest reaction often occurs during the first moments of entering cold water.

The immediate response is known as the cold-shock response. Sudden immersion can increase breathing, heart rate, and blood pressure as the body reacts to the abrupt temperature change.

What Happens to Your Heart Rate During a Cold Plunge?

When you suddenly enter cold water, your nervous system responds almost immediately. The initial cold-shock response can cause rapid breathing and an increase in heart rate.

Your heart-rate response does not necessarily remain the same throughout the plunge. It can vary with water temperature, immersion depth, breathing, whether the face is submerged, cold-water experience, and individual physiology.

For that reason, it is more accurate to say that cold water changes cardiovascular activity than to say that it always raises or always lowers heart rate.

Does a Cold Plunge Lower Blood Pressure?

A cold plunge should not be considered a quick way to lower blood pressure.

Sudden cold exposure causes blood vessels near the skin to constrict. This increases resistance to blood flow and can produce an acute increase in blood pressure.

This is one reason cold-water immersion deserves additional caution in people who already have high blood pressure or cardiovascular disease.

Research into repeated cold exposure and long-term cardiovascular adaptation is still developing, so an immediate blood-pressure response should not be confused with claims about possible long-term effects.

Why Does Cold Water Affect the Heart?

Cold water creates an immediate stress response. Blood vessels constrict and the sympathetic nervous system becomes more active as the body attempts to preserve heat and maintain core temperature.

These changes can increase the workload placed on the cardiovascular system during the initial exposure.

The colder the water and the more abrupt the immersion, the more intense the initial sensation may be.

Why Can Heart Rate Decrease in Cold Water?

Cold-water physiology is more complicated than a simple increase in heart rate.

Cooling or immersing the face can activate a diving response involving the parasympathetic nervous system, which can slow heart rate. At the same time, cold exposure to the body can activate the sympathetic cold-shock response.

Because these responses can occur together, heart rate during cold-water immersion can vary rather than following one predictable pattern.

This is also one reason deliberately combining very cold water, face immersion, and breath-holding should not be treated casually.

Can Cold Water Cause Heart Palpitations?

Some people notice a pounding, racing, or unusually noticeable heartbeat during sudden cold exposure. Cold water produces rapid changes in the autonomic nervous system and cardiovascular system, which can make the heartbeat feel more noticeable.

That does not mean every sensation is harmless.

If you develop a new or persistent irregular heartbeat, severe palpitations, fainting, chest pain, or significant difficulty breathing, stop the exposure and seek appropriate medical care.

Can a Cold Plunge Cause a Heart Attack?

Cold plunging does not mean a healthy person's heart will suddenly "explode," but sudden cold exposure does place additional stress on the cardiovascular system.

The American Heart Association notes that cold shock causes a rapid increase in breathing, heart rate, and blood pressure and makes the heart work harder. People with a history of cardiovascular disease may be at greater risk from abrupt cold-water immersion.

Anyone with known heart disease, a history of serious arrhythmias, or other significant cardiovascular concerns should discuss cold-water immersion with a healthcare professional before using it as a wellness practice.

Is Cold Plunging Dangerous for Your Heart?

For healthy people, brief cold-water exposure and prolonged accidental cold-water immersion are very different situations. Still, cold water creates real cardiovascular stress even when the exposure is voluntary.

Risk depends on factors including temperature, duration, how suddenly you enter the water, underlying health conditions, medications, and whether you submerge your face or hold your breath.

Do not use discomfort as a challenge to overcome. Leave the water if you experience concerning symptoms or lose control of your breathing or movement.

When to Stop a Cold Plunge

Exit the water if you develop symptoms such as:

  • Chest pain or pressure
  • Fainting or feeling close to fainting
  • Severe or persistent heart palpitations
  • Significant difficulty breathing
  • Confusion
  • Loss of coordination or physical control

Severe or persistent cardiovascular symptoms should not be dismissed as a normal part of cold plunging.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does cold plunging lower your heart rate?

Not necessarily. Sudden cold-water immersion can initially increase heart rate. Heart rate can change as immersion continues and may also be affected by breathing and facial cooling.

Does cold plunging lower blood pressure?

Not during the initial cold-shock response. Sudden cold exposure can constrict blood vessels and temporarily increase blood pressure. Cold plunging should not be used as a substitute for evidence-based blood-pressure treatment.

Why is my heart pounding during a cold plunge?

The initial cold-shock response activates the body's stress response and can increase heart rate and breathing. If pounding or palpitations are severe, persistent, irregular, or accompanied by other concerning symptoms, stop the plunge and seek medical advice.

Should people with high blood pressure cold plunge?

Because cold exposure can acutely increase blood pressure, people with hypertension should discuss cold-water immersion with a healthcare professional, particularly if their blood pressure is not well controlled or they have other cardiovascular conditions.
