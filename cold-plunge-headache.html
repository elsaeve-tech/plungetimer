Headache After a Cold Plunge

Updated August 2026

A headache during or after a cold plunge may be related to sudden cold exposure, rapid breathing, muscle tension, direct cooling of the head or face, or staying in water that is too cold for too long.

A brief, mild headache may improve after you exit the water and warm up. However, a sudden severe headache, a headache accompanied by neurological symptoms, or pain that does not improve should not be dismissed as a normal part of cold plunging.

What to Do Right Now

  • Exit the water safely.
  • Dry off and put on warm clothing.
  • Allow your body to warm gradually.
  • Sit or lie down if you feel dizzy or faint.
  • Do not begin another cold session while symptoms remain.

Can a Cold Plunge Cause a Headache?

Yes. Sudden immersion in cold water creates an immediate cold shock response that can rapidly alter breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, and the body's stress response. Some people may experience head pressure, aching, or a sharp cold-stimulus sensation during or shortly after the plunge.

Headaches are not inevitable, and researchers have not established one single explanation for every headache that occurs after cold immersion. The likely cause may differ depending on whether the pain begins immediately, appears after leaving the water, affects the forehead or temples, or occurs with dizziness or other symptoms.

Why Does My Head Hurt After a Cold Plunge?

1. The Initial Cold Shock Response

Sudden cold-water immersion can trigger an involuntary gasp, increased breathing, a faster heart rate, and a temporary rise in blood pressure. The abrupt physiological stress may contribute to headache or pressure sensations in susceptible people.

2. Rapid or Uncontrolled Breathing

The first moments of immersion may cause rapid breathing or hyperventilation. Overbreathing can reduce carbon dioxide levels in the blood and may lead to lightheadedness, tingling, or headache. Trying to hold your breath can also make the experience less controlled.

3. Direct Cold Exposure to the Head or Face

Submerging the face, scalp, or back of the neck may provoke a cold-stimulus headache similar to brain freeze. Keeping your head above water may help determine whether direct cooling is triggering the pain.

4. Neck, Jaw, or Shoulder Tension

Many people instinctively lift their shoulders, clench their jaw, or stiffen their neck when entering cold water. This muscle tension may produce or aggravate a tension-type headache.

5. Water That Is Too Cold

Near-freezing water creates a much more intense response than moderately cold water. You do not need to use the coldest possible temperature. A milder temperature may provide a more controlled experience with fewer symptoms.

6. Staying in Too Long

Colder water generally calls for a shorter session. Headache, uncontrolled shivering, numbness, confusion, or worsening discomfort may indicate that the exposure has exceeded your current tolerance.

7. An Existing Headache or Migraine Tendency

People with a history of migraine or other recurring headaches may be more sensitive to cold stimuli. Cold exposure may not affect everyone in the same way, so a routine that feels manageable to one person may provoke symptoms in another.

How to Prevent a Headache During a Cold Plunge

If your headaches are mild and resolve after the session, try reducing the intensity of your next plunge rather than attempting to push through the pain.

  • Use warmer water: Begin with a moderately cold temperature instead of ice-filled or near-freezing water.
  • Shorten the session: Reduce your exposure and increase it only when shorter sessions feel controlled.
  • Enter gradually: Avoid jumping into very cold water without giving yourself time to control your breathing.
  • Keep your head above water: Do not submerge your face or scalp if direct cold exposure appears to trigger pain.
  • Relax your upper body: Lower your shoulders, unclench your jaw, and avoid stiffening your neck.
  • Control your breathing: Focus on a slow exhale rather than breath-holding or rapid, forceful breathing.
  • Avoid plunging when you already feel unwell: Skip the session if you are already experiencing a significant headache, dizziness, illness, or unusual fatigue.

Should You Keep Cold Plunging if It Gives You a Headache?

Do not remain in the water to prove that you can tolerate the pain. Exit the plunge and allow the symptoms to settle. If you decide to try cold exposure again, use warmer water, keep your head out, and shorten the session.

Stop cold plunging and speak with a healthcare professional if the headaches are frequent, becoming more intense, lasting for a long time, or occurring with dizziness, faintness, visual changes, heart symptoms, or neurological symptoms.

When to Stop a Cold Plunge Immediately

Leave the water immediately if you experience any of the following:

  • A sudden or extremely severe headache
  • Dizziness, faintness, or loss of balance
  • Confusion or disorientation
  • Weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking
  • Vision changes
  • Chest pain or unusual chest pressure
  • Severe shortness of breath
  • Breathing that you cannot bring under control
  • Intense or uncontrollable shivering
  • Loss of coordination or difficulty exiting the water

A sudden severe headache—particularly one that feels unlike your usual headaches—requires urgent medical evaluation. Call emergency services for severe symptoms, fainting, chest pain, neurological changes, or trouble breathing.

Can Cold Plunges Trigger Migraines?

Cold stimuli can provoke head pain, and studies of cold-stimulus headaches suggest that people with a history of migraine may be more susceptible than people without a recurring headache history. However, this does not mean every person with migraine will react badly to cold immersion.

If you experience migraines, consider starting with warmer water, avoiding head and face immersion, and ending the session at the first sign of headache symptoms. Speak with your clinician before continuing if cold exposure repeatedly triggers migraine attacks.

Why Does My Head Hurt After an Ice Bath?

An ice bath and a cold plunge can cause similar symptoms because both expose the body to sudden cold. Ice baths may be especially intense when the water approaches freezing or when large amounts of ice remain against the skin.

Possible contributors include the cold shock response, rapid breathing, muscle tension, direct cooling of the face or neck, and an exposure time that is too long for the water temperature.

How Long Should You Stay in a Cold Plunge?

There is no single ideal time for every person. Water temperature, previous experience, body size, health conditions, and individual cold tolerance all affect the response.

If you develop headaches, treat that as a reason to reduce the intensity. Use a shorter duration, choose warmer water, and avoid immersing your head. Longer and colder sessions are not necessarily more beneficial.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a mild headache after a cold plunge normal?

A brief mild headache can occur, but it should not be treated as a required or desirable part of cold exposure. Exit the water and reduce the temperature or duration if it happens repeatedly.

Can putting my head underwater cause the headache?

It may. Direct cooling of the face, scalp, or neck can provoke cold-stimulus head pain in susceptible people. Keep your head above water and see whether that prevents the symptom.

Can breathing too fast cause a headache?

Rapid breathing or hyperventilation can contribute to headache, tingling, and lightheadedness. Exit the water if you cannot regain control of your breathing.

Should I take pain medication and continue plunging?

Do not use pain medication simply to mask symptoms so that you can remain in the water. Repeated or significant headaches deserve evaluation rather than being pushed through.

How long should a cold plunge headache last?

A minor cold-stimulus headache may improve shortly after the cold exposure stops. Seek medical advice when pain is severe, unusual, persistent, worsening, recurring frequently, or accompanied by other concerning symptoms.
